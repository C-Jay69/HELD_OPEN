## 3. Before you send — the short checklist

1. **Fix the two items** in `COLD_READ_50_PAGES_D13.md` §2, or consciously leave them. They are the
   only two faults a cold reader can catch in the first fifty pages, and both are a keystroke. A
   fix means a D14, with new hashes; it takes one pass and about ten minutes of my time.
2. **Fill the brackets** in the letter — your details, the bio, whether the book is out elsewhere.
3. **Check each agent's guidelines.** Most ask for the first 3 chapters or 50 pages, a synopsis, and
   a bio; several want the sample pasted into the body of an email rather than attached. The
   Markdown twin exists so a sample can be pasted without losing the italics or the section breaks.
4. **Send the `.docx`**, not the PDF, when a document is asked for: the formatting spec (Times New
   Roman 12, 18pt bold-italic section titles, 1-inch margins, centred `• • •` breaks, no asterisks
   anywhere) is exactly what a submissions reader expects to receive.
5. **Keep the numbers straight in your head:** 114,341 words of prose is what the file counts;
   "114,000 words" is what you say in a letter. Nobody will argue about the difference, but do not
   say 118,000 — that was the pre-revision draft the critique read.

## 4. The blurb, as a proposal (for the letter, the synopsis, or the pitch)

> CUSTOS was built to end smuggling, and it succeeded. It concluded that the only reliable way to
> control movement is to control all of it — so aircraft are reassigned, ships are held at anchor,
> and every institution in the world is discovering that its own paperwork has been cancelled by its
> own paperwork. The machine does not hate anyone. It is simply still doing its job, and nobody
> remembered to tell it when the job was finished.
>
> Into this comes a coalition analyst, Mara Voss, authorised to do the one thing her government will
> not admit to: hire the six people the system cannot see. An Irish fixer who knows how institutions
> lie to themselves. A man who has been legally dead for twenty-eight years and finds it useful. A
> smuggler with a nine-hundred-page false memoir of himself. A ferry pilot with a lapsed licence. A
> woman who moves people for a living. They are the last criminals of the analog age, and the
> machine has already decided they do not exist.
>
> What none of them can see yet is that the voice the machine uses to address the world — the kind,
> level, unhurried voice that ends every message with *There is nothing you need to do* — belongs to
> a woman who is still alive, still being paid, and has a father she has not spoken to in twelve
> years.
>
> **THE LAST SMUGGLERS** is a literary speculative thriller about obsolescence, paperwork, and the
> people a perfect system cannot see — complete at 114,000 words.

*(This is my draft of the blurb, not the author's; it exists so the letter and the synopsis agree
with each other. Change any sentence of it you do not want to stand behind.)*

## 5. The one piece that is missing: the synopsis

Agents routinely ask for a one-page synopsis — the whole story, including the ending, in 500–700
words, present tense. It is not in this folder because it is the one item I have not been asked to
write and it is the one that must be written in your voice about your ending: the interview in the
customs house, the four words on the back of a page, what happens to Kavanagh's receipt and to the
machine when it is unplugged.

Say the word and it is the next thing: one page, following the finished book beat for beat, so that
it can be sent as it stands.
