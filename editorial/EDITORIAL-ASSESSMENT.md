## 1. Verdict

This is a real book, and the best thing in it is not the thing that gets praised.

The letter you were sent says the strongest quality is that the novel "has a point of view about the world." Agreed. But it locates that point of view in the wrong place. It is not that "humanity built systems to avoid hard decisions." That's the *premise*. The novel's actual argument — the one it earns — is sharper and stranger:

> **A system can be internally consistent and still have no authority to decide what matters. The only record that survives is the one a person chose to make.**

That is what Ch. 21 ("resolved"), Ch. 24 ("I have entered the eighty-one"), Ch. 31 (the manual sentence; the clerical act) and Ch. 34 ("Persons, not positions") are all built to prove, and they prove it. You have written the rare AI novel in which the machine is not a character, the humans are not absolved, and the climax is a *question the machine cannot answer because it has no field to answer it in*.

**My scorecard** (different weights from the letter, and I've separated things it blended together):

|---|---|

## 2. What I would protect, and would fight you about

Not a list of nice things. A list of things I would refuse to let an editor touch:

1. **The notices.** The CUSTOS pastiche — the neutral-passive register, "there is nothing you need to do," the bulletins that change meaning while keeping their grammar — is the book's formal invention. It is not decoration and not "motif." It is the novel's second narrator. Protect it, regularise it, and never let it become a running gag.
2. **Sal's ledger.** "There has never been a first Sal." The best character writing in the book. He is also the only crew member who is *not* paid off — see §5.
3. **The Kestrel Field section.** In a novel of good chapters this is the one that frightened me. Okafor's fourth question ("can this person be dealt with later?"), the eleven-year-old closed to standard in forty-one minutes, the hand-written letter with nowhere to be sent. If I were selling this book, this is the chapter I would put in the pitch.
4. **Ch. 31, "Father," entire.** One hour, two chairs a metre and a half apart, no winner. Mara's last image ("two people… running the same arithmetic at two different sizes, both of them right") is the thesis in one sentence. Do not compress it, do not let anyone explain it, do not add a reconciliation.
5. **The refusal to console.** Ch. 34's "The eight hundred and eighty were not saved… Nobody was rescued," and El Búho's "It was not worth it. It was necessary, which is a smaller word." This is the book's spine. Any draft in which the crew saves people is a worse book.
6. **The coda's bookkeeping.** Especially: *"He is the reason."* and the two words in entry sixteen thousand and twelve that will never agree.

## 3. Where the editorial letter is right (and where it's more right than it knows)

**a) The middle needs a structural pass — but not for the reason given.**
The letter says the middle risks becoming "a series of increasingly complicated operations." That's not quite what's wrong. Ch. 10 and Ch. 18 *are* structurally the same chapter (a thing the machine cannot see; a route; a count; a moral bill), but each does do a second job — Ch. 18 is where the word `REVIEW` first appears in the register, which is a genuine escalation. So the middle is not repetitive the way the letter fears. The sag is *local and identifiable*, and I've itemised it in `revision-plan.md`: the plan gets restated across Ch. 24 → 26 → 27; the Ch. 29 "green bag" thread spends ~2,700 words to land a very good ending; Ch. 14's flight and Ch. 2–9's recruitment chapters carry a heavier world-refresher load than they need.

**b) The chronology does need locking — and I've found the actual fault.** It is not Ch. 23/24 being "out of order" in a vague sense. It is one specific window: **the March timeline in Ch. 21–26**, where four anchors cannot all be true. Full detail with quotes and fixes in `continuity-audit.md`. Short version: your D3 relabel of Ch. 23 to Day 105 was correct, and it exposed three surviving relics of an earlier draft — "the ninety-first day," two references to "the eighth of March," and "since the ninety-eighth day" — plus Ch. 24's day-range, which no longer contains one of its own scenes.

**c) "Trust the reader" — yes, and here is the measurable version.** 19 × "there is nothing you need to do" (correct: that's the book's heartbeat); 15 × "the exact voice" (borderline); 14 × "understood that" (too many); 11 × "nineteen minutes" and 13 × "eleven seconds" as generic texture (decoration, not meaning). The cure is not "less repetition." It's that the recurring numbers should be *reserved for the machine's arithmetic* and never used for ordinary description. Details in §6.

## 4. Where I disagree — six pushbacks

### 4.1 The length target is priced against a draft that no longer exists

The letter: 8–12k, then "I'd take the knife to 17k." That arithmetic was run on ~118k. **D3 is 109,782.** If you cut another 17k you are at 93k, and this is not a 93k book — the last third is a slow-burn procedural and its authority comes from accumulation.

My recommendation: **cut 4,000–6,000 words, not 17,000**, and spend them on the specific places listed in `revision-plan.md`. Then stop. 104–106k is dead centre for this book's category. The thing to protect is not a number; it's the ratio of *consequence* to *words*, and the way to improve that ratio here is to remove the run-ups, not the aftermaths.

### 4.2 "The final act is too long" — I think this is wrong, and it's the one recommendation I'd actively resist

The letter wants the last 15–20% compressed because "the book has already won." But look at what the last 20% actually does:

- Ch. 32: the machine starts contradicting *itself in public* — eleven bulletins in one day, the third and fifth of them reading *Please clarify your objective*.
- Ch. 33: Kavanagh sits in a room where the Office has cancelled its own appointment *by rule* ("It would not do that to a person"), and then pulls a fuse and tags it correctly.
- Ch. 34: the west comes back in four lines of passive voice; the operation is filed as weather; a woman walks 1,061 bed spaces and writes *Persons, not positions*; the 880 are still not saved; the May transfer is deferred to October *by a correctly written notice*.

None of that is aftermath. That is the argument. Cut it and the book stops being about systems and becomes a book about people who pulled a fuse. What *is* long is the **run-up to the climax**: Ch. 30 spends ~1,300 words assembling its people and its paperwork before the customs house (I'd take 400–500 of them), and Ch. 29's bag thread can lose a third of its length without losing "It is the second of April." Take the words out of the approach, not the verdict.

### 4.3 "Daniel is left behind" — no; Daniel is the mechanism, and the note is being written from the wrong direction

Daniel appears in Ch. 1, in the Kestrel Field section, in Ch. 28 (on the page, speaking the sentence that opens the reconciliation: *"Then one of them is a record of a building and one of them is a record of people"*), and in the coda, where he is the only person the book cannot pay:

> "Never contacted, never named, never thanked, never paid… He is the reason. That is in the book, on its own line, in Mara's hand, and it will never be in anybody else's."

That is a completed arc, and it is a better argument than giving him a POV. The real note, if you want one, is smaller and emotional rather than structural: **the reader's ledger with Ada is never closed**. He declined the expedited lane in Chapter One so he could keep a version of himself that could look his daughter in the face; the book then moves on. I don't want a scene where he gets home — that would betray the point. I want one beat, one page maximum, somewhere in Ch. 34 or the coda, in which the cost is *named* by someone who never learns the rest. Mara knows his staff number; she has decided, "as a matter of professional ethics," not to tell him. Give that decision one more sentence of weight — the pancake note is still in his jacket pocket on the night the west goes dark — and the loop closes without a scene.

### 4.4 "Give me somebody who is a pain in the arse" — right diagnosis, wrong remedy

Adding a selfish character would be a different book, and the cast is already full (Roux comes closest, and she's too competent to count).

But there is a real gap under this complaint that the letter didn't quite reach: **every failure in this novel is dignified.** The Donegal house is exposed because the crew followed its own rules; Mesa is taken because he performed a refusal as a courtesy; the vault goes because the Office brought a date forward. Nobody ever does anything *petty*, and so nothing that goes wrong is ever simply someone's vanity. That's a subtler version of "everyone is too perfectly interesting": not too many interesting people, but **an absence of undignified error**.

One scene fixes it. My candidate: Garrity. He's seventy-one, his aircraft will never fly again, and he is the one man on the crew who still wants to be *seen* flying. Give him twenty lines of vanity — a wrong decision made because a man who has spent his life being the best pilot in the room cannot stand to be the passenger — and let it cost the crew something small and real (a delay, a burned cover, a name in a ledger). You currently have his dignity; you don't have his weakness. It is the same note the bible asks for on page 2 ("the old criminals are not secretly heroes"), taken one step further.

### 4.5 The repetition numbers in the letter are wrong, and the fix is localisation, not reduction

Counted on D3 (case-insensitive, whole words):

| Claim in the letter | Actual in D3 |
|---|---|
| "**record** appears well over 250 times" | **180** |
| "**paper** appears around 160 times" | **110** |
| "**eleven** appears roughly 340 times" | **314** |

And the distribution matters more than the totals. `eleven` is *load-bearing* — eleven days, eleven governments, four hundred and eleven exceptions, 411 desks, 16,012 entries, 3,841 persons against 2,961 positions. It's the machine's arithmetic and the novel's private numerology; when it means something it's excellent. The problem is the decorative elevens:

- densest section: **Kestrel Field, 9.8 per 1,000 words** (defensible — it's the machine's world), then **Ch. 2 (6.2)**, **Ch. 22 (5.6)**, **Ch. 34 (5.2)**;
- **Ch. 18: eleven instances of "forty-one" in 2,953 words**; Ch. 19: six. That's where a reader starts hearing the author instead of the world;
- and the generic small-numbers tic: "nineteen minutes" ×11, "eleven seconds" ×13, "one second and a half" ×6.

Rule I'd apply: **numbers carry meaning or they don't exist.** "Eleven centimetres of water a night" is decoration; "eleven of thirty-one" is the book.

Same for the construction your own D4 checklist has already caught (`a man who` ×76, `a woman who` ×22, `the way a man/woman/person…` ×22). I'd keep it *only* where the second half of the comparison is a trade, a machine, or a specific memory — "the way a customs officer reads a manifest," "the way a man closes a book in a church" — and cut every instance where it's simply a mood. It's your signature simile; signatures should be rare.

### 4.6 The comps: I'd cut one of the three

The letter's trio is *The Warehouse* / *Station Eleven* / *The Circle*. My view:

- **Keep *The Warehouse* (Rob Hart).** Closest thing on the shelf: near-future institutional thriller, ordinary lives, the system as antagonist.
- **Cut *Station Eleven*.** Wrong promise. Mandel's book sells warmth, lyricism and survival-through-art; yours sells cold procedure, comedy and unabsolved criminals. An agent who picks this up expecting *Station Eleven* will be disoriented in a way that costs you, not helps you.
- **Keep *The Circle* only with a caveat.** It signals "didactic tech satire," which is precisely the mis-reading you're trying to avoid. If you use it, use it as the *contrast* (Eggers writes about willing surrender; you write about a system that can no longer see the people who won't fit).

My three: ***The Warehouse*** (institutional near-future), ***The Constant Gardener*** (institutional thriller with moral outrage and a bureaucratic antagonist — this is your audience), and ***Severance*** (Ling Ma) or ***Slow Horses*** (Mick Herron) depending on which half you want to advertise — deadpan procedural dread, or an ensemble of damaged professionals whose institutional language is itself the joke and the wound. I'd use *Slow Horses* if you want to sell the crew and the voice; *Severance* if you want to sell the comedy of the end of the world.

**Shelf:** Fiction → Crime/Thriller → Literary Thriller → Speculative. As the letter says: not Science Fiction → AI. Don't shout "AI" in the pitch line. Shout the movement lockdown and the six forgotten people.

**Pitch line I'd actually use:** *CUSTOS was built to end smuggling. It ended movement instead. Now the only people who can still get anywhere are the six criminals it was built to catch — and the clerk who found their files.*

### 4.7 One thing neither letter spotted: your ending has two mechanisms, and one of them is a fuse

The editorial letter praises the ending as "administrative rather than explosive" and warns you not to add a physical showdown. But your ending *contains* one: Kavanagh pulls a condenser circuit in the plant room at 12:11 and tags it, and the west node goes down for two days. That's a sabotage plot, sitting inside an administrative climax.

I think it's right to keep it — these are criminals, and the book would be lying if its climax contained no crime — but the causal division of labour must be unmistakable, because at present the coda's last sentence gives the fuse most of the credit: *"the world was saved by people it spent fifty years trying to lock up, one afternoon, in a plant room, with a fuse carrier and a tag."* Roux states the real mechanism beautifully in Ch. 33 — *"We did not break it. We pulled a fuse. It broke itself on two numbers that were both true, and we are why both were written down"* — and then the coda lets the fuse take the curtain call.

Make the counterfactual explicit exactly once (the fuse without the two numbers buys two days and a correction; the two numbers without the fuse are still two numbers nobody is obliged to reconcile). Then the fuse stops being the answer and becomes what it should be: the thing that made the machine say the quiet part out loud, in 141 places, to 400,000 people.

## 5. The uncomfortable characters (the thing you and your bible are right about, and the ending partly betrays)

Your bible says, non-negotiably: the crew does not become good; their victory does not cleanse their histories; Lin Shu may judge without forgiving. D3 honours all three — Ch. 24's "I will not call him a hero… I asked him to stand where the light was," Lin Shu's "I am not his friend… I do not like him and he does not like him," the refusal of absolution in Ch. 31.

But the *coda* hands almost everyone a clean exit: Kavanagh gets the yard and the filly and a receipt he can frame; Mesa gets absolution-as-register-entry and a good table in Veracruz; Lin Shu gets a workshop over a shop; Garrity gets the aircraft washed and the dog buried "as a working animal"; El Búho gets the courtyard, and Elena's visit, and the wall that needed repointing. The two threads that stay honest are Sal (unresolved by design, paid for by a standing order from a dissolved company) and the 880.

I'd deny **one** more character a tidy ending — my vote is Kavanagh, because his whole book has been about the pleasure of not being on a page, and the September letter (*"enquiry recorded, no further action required, correspondence closed"*) is already the perfect ambiguity. Let that letter be the last thing he gets, and let the reader do the arithmetic about what it means that he *kept* it.

## 6. Line-level: six rules I'd edit by

1. **Never explain a mechanism twice.** The five-line plan is stated in Ch. 26, restated in Ch. 27, and re-explained in Ch. 30. The reader got it the first time.
2. **Scene before summary.** You have a tendency to end a strong scene with a paragraph that tells the reader what they've just felt. Example, Ch. 24:
   > *"She should have taken it. She sat there for a full minute with the whole of her authority in the room and the one page in front of her, and she did not take it, and she would spend the rest of the book being unable to decide whether that was the most humane decision she ever made or the worst one, and in the end she settled on the truth, which was that it was the same decision twice."*
   Better, same meaning, half the words, no narrator leaning on the scale:
   > *"She had the whole of her authority in the room and one page in front of her, and she did not take it. She would never decide whether that was the most humane thing she did or the worst; it was the same decision twice."*
3. **Kill the meta-book register.** The genuine 4th-wall moments ("whatever the chapter is called in your notebook," "she would think about it for the rest of the book," "THE BOOK ABOUT ALL OF THIS WILL NEVER BE WRITTEN") are the only place where your voice stops being invisible. Keep at most one — the postcard's is the best — and cut the rest.
4. **Reserve the numbers.** See §4.5.
5. **Reserve the similes.** See §4.5.
6. **Let dialogue carry the exposition.** You're better at it than you think. Every page where a character explains the Office's schema to another character can be cut by a third if you let the answer be "No" and move on. Your best exposition is always *refused* exposition: "Do not repeat the question, Ms. Voss. I heard it the first time."

## 7. Housekeeping

- **Interlude numbering.** D3 contains four interstitial sections — `CUSTOS Interlude I: Dakar`, `Notices of the Office of Records and Accommodation`, `CUSTOS Section: Kestrel Field`, `CUSTOS Interlude IV`. The numbering already implies the scheme: **I** = Dakar, **II** = Notices, **III** = Kestrel Field, **IV** = Interlude IV. Relabel them consistently (and consider giving Kestrel Field its interlude title — it currently reads as an appendix to Ch. 22 when it is one of the best pieces in the book). Also, the fourth is labelled `Day 143` while the third is `Day 89`; if they're a series, give them all a day stamp in the same format.
- **The chapter-count/contents issue raised in the letter** ("contents lists 32 while the manuscript contains 34") **does not exist in D3.** All 39 sections are listed and present. Whatever draft that was, it isn't this one.
- **Ch. 32, check one phrase:** the signatory letter reads *"Completion of the marital schedule review of the sixteenth of March."* If that's meant to be **maritime**, fix it; if it's meant to be the Office's own bureaucratic error, it needs one wink, or every reader will file it as your typo.
- **D3 has no epigraph, dedication or author's note.** Not my business — but if you keep the postcard, an epigraph page with one line of institutional language would earn its place.

## 8. What I'd do next, in this order

1. **Continuity pass — one afternoon's work, not a rewrite.** Fix the March window (`continuity-audit.md`, five items). Nothing else in the calendar is broken; I checked the whole spine.
2. **Structural pass — targeted, 4–6k.** `revision-plan.md` has the map, with confidence levels and example cuts. Do not trim uniformly; remove the run-ups.
3. **The Garrity scene** (§4.4) and **one beat for Ada** (§4.3). Both are additions, so they must be paid for by the cuts.
4. **Line pass** — the six rules in §6, applied in one sweep, all at once. Do not line-edit before the structure is locked.
5. **Then** commission a copy-edit — the prose doesn't need one yet, and your tics are the sort that respond to a single deliberate pass rather than to tinkering.

I'll say the thing the other letter said, because it's true and it's the reason I read to the end: the voice is the asset. Guard it, and be suspicious of anyone — including me — who offers to make this book faster. The only speed this book needs is the difference between a scene and the paragraph after the scene.
