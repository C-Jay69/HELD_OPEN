## A. The calendar works, and it is more precise than the draft's own labels

D3 never names a year, but it runs a strict day-number system, and two in-text calendar anchors fix it:

- **Ch. 28 is "Day 122 — the second of April."**
- **Ch. 31 is "Day 141 — the twenty-first of April."** (122 + 19 = 141 ✓)

Therefore **Day 0 = the first of December**, and:

| Day | Date | Where it's used |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1 Dec | Ch. 1 (the flight) |
| 11 | 12 Dec | Ch. 2 (the annex) |
| 12 | 13 Dec | Ch. 3 (Kavanagh; "December light" ✓) |
| 89 | 28 Feb | Kestrel Field ("The boy came up on **Thursday**" — 28 Feb is a Thursday if Day 0 is a Saturday ✓) |
| 97 | 8 Mar | Ch. 24 opens |
| 105 | **16 Mar** | Ch. 23 ("the sixteenth"; the port interview; the collection begins) |
| 122 | 2 Apr | Ch. 28 (the movement; the register line) |
| 124 | 4 Apr | the van takes Sal (Ch. 29) |
| 141 | 21 Apr | Ch. 30/31 (Elena; the hour) |
| 156 | 6 May | Kavanagh's cancelled 11:00 interview; the fuse pulled at 12:11 (Ch. 33) |
| 158 | 8 May | the west restored; Ch. 34 opens ("the eighth of May") |
| 189 | 8 Jun | Ch. 34's closing movement ("the eighth of June") ✓ |

Everything in that table is internally consistent, including the two-day outage (6th–8th May) and the June movement. **The spine is sound.** What follows is one tangled window and a handful of relics.

## B. The March window — where the timeline actually breaks

D3 changed Ch. 23's stamp from **Days 94–96** (D1 and the full-manuscript docx both say 94–96) to **Day 105**. That change is a **correction**: Ch. 23's content is the 16th of March — the port completion interview, the collection lorries at the gate, El Búho's "no version of the sixteenth of March in which that man walks out of a port office…" (Ch. 22). Day 105 = 16 March. Good.

But the correction left the rest of the window behind, and now four anchors cannot all be true:

### FLAG 1 — Ch. 22's arithmetic doesn't reach the sixteenth (confidence: high)

> "The application went on the **ninety-first day**." (Day 91 = 2 March)
> "**Four days** for the category review. **Two more** to generate the appointment." … "**The sixteenth.** A designated office at the Port of Dublin, at twenty to ten in the morning."

91 + 6 = **Day 97 (8 March)**, not Day 105. This is the root of the whole window: the schedule was presumably once the 8th, then moved to the 16th so the port interview would fall on the same day the vault collection begins — and the arithmetic and the two letter dates were left where they were.

**Fix (cheapest):** keep the application on Day 91 and change the estimate to *"Eleven days for the category review. Three more to generate the appointment."* — or move the application to Day 99. Either way, one sentence.

### FLAG 2 — "the eighth of March," twice (confidence: high)

- Ch. 24, the Office's letter: *"After the interview on the eighth of March, the Office wrote to them."* and *"a person was interviewed on the eighth of March and has been entered in the record as a respondent."*
- Ch. 25, Kavanagh's receipt: *"his request of the eighth of March had been recorded."*

Two different events are being dated 8 March, and neither comfortably is:
- Mesa's **completion** interview is the 16th (Ch. 23). An *intake* interview at the western facility on the 8th is plausible and useful — it is what explains "declined to provide particulars of next of kin" and "he is accommodated pending completion of the record" — but the letter must read as a distinct, earlier event, not as the port interview.
- Kavanagh's **request** (the category review he signs) went in on Day 91 (2 March) per Ch. 22. The receipt should say so.

**Fix:** in Ch. 24, keep the eighth but make it explicit ("interviewed at the western facility on the eighth of March"); in Ch. 25, change the receipt to the date the application was actually filed, then make sure FLAG 1's arithmetic lands on a date that matches.

### FLAG 3 — Ch. 24's day range no longer contains one of its own scenes (confidence: high)

The vault collection starts on **the 16th**:
- Ch. 21 (Days 77–85): the notice *"Reserve Facility 4-C… Collection, week commencing the sixteenth of March,"* brought forward from the 30th;
- Ch. 23's closing scene: two lorries and a gang of nine at the north gate on the 16th, *"waiting to be told where the microfilm was. Rask had stopped them at the gate on paperwork, which he could do for one day and no longer."*

But **Ch. 24 is stamped "Days 97–104"** and opens: *"For the first three days the collection looked exactly like a collection,"* with lorries going down the lane at seven with thirty-seven tonnes, and Sal signing a manifest every evening at six. Those scenes are the 17th–19th of March at the earliest (Days 106–108).

**Fix (recommended):** re-stamp Ch. 24 as **"Days 97–108"** and insert one scene-divider in the middle of the chapter (the draft already uses `---` dividers), so the *shape* is explicit: the 8 March letter and the argument, then the collection days. Alternatively move the collection/confrontation material into Ch. 26 (Days 108–115) and leave Ch. 24 as pure confession — but the burning, the confrontation and the confession are one continuous arc, and I'd rather re-stamp than break it.

### FLAG 4 — "since the ninety-eighth day" (confidence: high)

Ch. 26, Sal: *"My application has been under a mattress since the **ninety-eighth day**."* In Ch. 24, Mara takes the letter on the **third evening of the collection** — i.e. Day 107/108 if FLAG 3 is fixed, Day 99/100 under the old dating. Either way the number must match whatever you settle on. (Note the same sentence's follow-through is excellent and should be kept: *"Two weeks… I will send it myself on 2 April, with the movement."*)

### FLAG 5 — reading order vs story order: Ch. 23 (Day 105) before Ch. 24 (Days 97–104) (confidence: medium — it may be deliberate)

As printed, the reader goes: the 16th of March (the port, the completion, the lorries) → back to the 8th of March and the days before it → forward again to the 16th–18th (Ch. 25, Days 105–107). In D1/D2 this was linear (23 = 94–96). If you *want* the port scene first — and I think you should, it's the best hinge in the book — signal it. One dateline will do:

> **Chapter Twenty-Three — Day 105 — the sixteenth of March**
> **Chapter Twenty-Four — Days 97–104 — eight days earlier**

The information is all there; only the reader's confidence is missing.

### FLAG 6 — "two hundred and eleven" vs "411" in the same chapter (confidence: high — a slip)

Ch. 24 and Ch. 27 both say Sal burned **211** items ("Two hundred and eleven items… Every one of them in the aisle where the north wall weeps"; Ch. 27: "two hundred and eleven items of ash in a barrow"). But Ch. 26 has him say: *"I have **411 items of ash**, and I know every call number; I wrote them."* 411 is your other motif number (Crane's notebook entries; the desks; the June movement); 211 is the ash. Pick one and make them different on purpose — the coincidence is currently invisible and reads like a typo.

### FLAG 7 — the two 880s (confidence: medium — decide, don't leave)

- Ch. 33, El Búho: *"It cost a son, and a hip, and a girl's forearm, and **eight hundred and eighty people standing in a doorway in the rain** tonight because the doors came open."*
- Ch. 34, Roux: *"**Eight hundred and eighty people have just become persons.**"* — and 3,841 − 2,961 = **880**, exactly. That's airtight and beautiful.

So Ch. 34's 880 is the reconciled difference (the people who existed and were not counted). Ch. 33's 880 is spoken before anyone knows that number — unless El Búho means *the uncounted*, in which case he knows something he can't yet know, or it's the entire block standing in the rain, which is 3,841 people, not 880.

**My recommendation:** make it deliberate. Change Ch. 33's line to *"three thousand eight hundred and forty-one people standing in a doorway"* — then Ch. 34's "eight hundred and eighty people have just become persons" arrives as the difference between the two numbers, and the reader feels the arithmetic instead of hearing it. That single change converts an ambiguity into one of the best structural rhymes in the book.
