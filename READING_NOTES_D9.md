## 3. What the read found — one structural thing, and it is a good one to have found now

**All forty-two sections open on narration. Not one opens on a line of dialogue.**

I checked every one of them. It is not a defect — the openings are good — but it is monotony at the
level of the reader's rhythm, and it is invisible in a single chapter and unmistakable across a
book. You have a book with an exceptional ear for speech, and the reader never once arrives on a
voice. Opening a handful of chapters on the spoken line would cost nothing and change the way the
book feels to hold.

Candidates where the narration in front of the first speech is short and the speech is the real
opening — i.e. where the swap is one paragraph moving down a line:

| Ch | D9 text | the line it could open on |
|---|---|---|
| 20 — The Price | "They spent two days deciding…" | *"Two vehicles, one night, seventy-two hours, three people and a priest who will not lie for us."* |
| 22 — Ransom | "Roux said the thing that decided everything…" | *"A man in a facility is not in a cell. He is in a schedule."* |
| 33 — Father | "The door closed, the hum went on behind the wall…" | *"Whose chairs are those?"* |
| 35 — Please Clarify | — | the office's own bulletin line |
| 37 — Offline | — | *"So. A glass each. And then, in the morning, the boat."* |

I have **not** made this change, because it moves your prose around rather than fixing a mistake.

**A second rhythm note, softer:** thirty-six of the forty-two sections close on narration, and a lot
of those closings are a sentence that states what the scene means. The six that close on a spoken
line are the six that hit hardest. Same fix, same reasoning, same restraint: your call.
